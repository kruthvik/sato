# Evidence gaps across human-learning reviews: a bounded audit of boundaries and design risk

**Scope status: PARTIAL CORPUS AUDIT.** The project asks for twelve evidence reviews and two syntheses. Only reviews 02–08 are present in the supplied source trail; reviews 01 and 09–12 and syntheses 13–14 are absent [30]. This audit does not claim full 12+2 coverage. The missing materials could change conclusions.

## Executive summary

The strongest conclusion is not that one technique always wins, but that interventions affect particular delayed outcomes under specified conditions. Immediate practice performance, model fit, neural correlates, and engagement are not substitutes for delayed learning. Retrieval and spacing benefit many studied retention tasks, with meaningful qualifications: unsuccessful retrieval without correction may fail, procedural spacing results can be null, classroom spacing varies, and interleaving may reduce practice accuracy while improving later problem solving in a particular course [1–4, 6]. Meta-analytic averages and reviews should not be counted as independent replications without a study-overlap audit; the seven local reviews themselves flag overlap and the audit did not construct an overlap matrix [1,4,8,10,18,19,29].

**Design implication:** compare policies against fair, time-matched baselines and assess the target outcome after a meaningful delay. Distinguish practiced performance, delayed retention, conceptual explanation, retrieval accessibility, transfer, and procedural fluency. Do not infer durable learning from immediate accuracy, confidence, response-time models, biomarkers, or engagement alone [1–4, 12–14].

## How to read this audit

- **Established (bounded):** synthesized or repeated behavioral direction within named task families, not a universal guarantee.
- **Plausible inference:** consistent with evidence but not uniquely identified or necessarily transportable.
- **Active dispute:** results vary by comparator, task, delay, or population.
- **Unknown:** absent, underpowered, inaccessible, proxy-based, or blocked by missing reviews.

The four status groups below are the requested ranking: established/bounded findings first, then plausible inferences, active disputes, and unknowns. Within each group, items are ordered by relevance to instructional design and directness of delayed behavioral evidence. This is not a cross-method effect-size or confidence leaderboard: the physics RCT is included for its immediate–delayed discrepancy, not because a single study outranks a synthesis. Confidence is a qualitative scope judgment, not a formal risk-of-bias grade.

## Evidence-status ranking

### Tier 1 — Established findings, bounded to studied tasks

**E1. Retrieval practice often improves delayed retention over restudy, but success or correction matters.** R02’s evidence table (classroom quizzing, delayed retrieval vs restudy, and transfer rows) reports later-memory benefits and distinguishes practice accuracy from final retention [1; R02, Evidence table]. Across five no-feedback adult word-pair experiments (N=318; ages 21–76; final tests at 10 minutes, 24 hours, or one week), standard retrieval did not beat restudy when initial retrieval success was low; diminishing-cue retrieval outperformed both [2; Results, Experiments 1a–1e]. The study used a <50% initial-success condition motivated by a meta-analytic subgroup, not a universal learner threshold. In feedback or higher-success experiments, the pattern differed [2; Results, Experiments 2–3]. A randomized six-arm Chinese grade-10 carbon-cycle study (136 completers from 186 enrolled) found no immediate condition difference; after a 30-minute learning activity and at one week, retrieval-only favored restudy and some drawing/generation conditions on total/factual scores, but not application questions [3]. Factual retention must not be described as conceptual transfer. Confidence: moderate for this one-week factual-retention result; low for broad conceptual-transfer claims.

**E2. Spacing is well supported for many retention tasks, not every procedural or classroom outcome.** R03’s first evidence-table row summarizes the verbal-recall spacing synthesis (317 experiments, 184 articles, 839 assessments); its next row describes the ISI-by-retention-interval relation [4; R03, Evidence table, rows 1–2]. These sources support a direction across verbal-recall paradigms, not one universal classroom interval. A randomized computer-tutorial study of 235 university students learning a permutation procedure found no significant massed/1-day/11-day spacing effect at one or five weeks [5]. A 2024 single-paper synthesis across nine introductory STEM courses found significant course-level spacing benefits in two courses and small positive effects in two more; the pooled result was not significant with calculus excluded [6]. This qualifies course generality, not declarative-recall evidence. Qualitative confidence: high only for the direction in the repeatedly studied verbal-recall corpus summarized in R03; this audit did not recheck study overlap, publication-bias diagnostics, or pooled magnitude. Confidence is lower for classroom generality (moderate) and complex procedures (low-to-moderate).

**E3. Practice performance is not learning: a course study found an immediate–delayed reversal.** In a preregistered counterbalanced undergraduate physics-course study (350 participants; stage analyses n=290 and n=286), blocked homework scores were higher and learners rated interleaved practice harder/less effective; surprise tests on novel problems more than a week later favored interleaving in both stages (reported d=.40 and .91) [7; Results, stages 1–2]. This is direct delayed behavioral evidence in one setting, not proof that interleaving always works. Confidence: moderate for this course and test design, low for generalization to other domains.

### Tier 2 — Plausible inferences, not universal policies

**P1. Guidance should adapt to topic knowledge and task complexity.** A 2025 expertise-reversal meta-analysis summarized in the local review includes 60 experiments and 5,924 participants; the indexed summary reports high assistance favoring lower-prior-knowledge learners and lower assistance favoring higher-prior-knowledge learners, but full-text diagnostics were not independently checked [8; R04 evidence table, expertise-reversal row]. An apprentice study reports an expertise-reversal pattern for electrical-control problem solving, but its accessible ERIC record is abstract-only; sample and long-delay outcome are not verified [9]. Fading guidance after demonstrated component mastery is a design hypothesis, not a proven universal policy. A separate four-experiment study of testing after worked examples vs restudy found a one-week null in electrical troubleshooting (Experiment 1, N=120), showing that worked-example-plus-test is not automatically better [25]. Cognitive load and schema formation are proposed explanations, not uniquely established by these contrasts [8–9]. Confidence: moderate for an interaction in the studied task families; low for universal thresholds or delayed-transfer effects.

**P2. Structured productive failure is a plausible conditional strategy, not “failed attempt” alone.** A randomized experiment recruited 85 Swiss sixth-graders; brief algebra problem solving before instruction was compared with solving after instruction and assessed after one day and one week. The one-week procedural outcome favored pre-instruction attempts (reported F(1,72)=4.35, p=.041, ηp²=.06), while conceptual explanation and transfer were nonsignificant [13]. This is a near procedural result, not conceptual/far transfer. Productive-failure reviews define the intervention as generation followed by instruction/consolidation [8]. Confidence: moderate for carefully sequenced procedural/conceptual outcomes in studied designs; low for unsupported discovery or broad transfer.

**P3. Adaptive schedules may improve delayed recall, but broad superiority is not established.** In Mettler et al.’s randomized experiments (72 undergraduates in Experiment 1; 48 in Experiment 2), adaptive accuracy/response-time spacing outperformed fixed schedules on one-week tests of country names/locations [14]. A 2020 chemistry extension reports two-week retention and 2–3-month standardized chemistry transfer in its abstract, but is the same research program and full text was not independently available here [15]. A large app analysis uses many response events, but its principal forgetting-rate outcome is response-sequence-derived, not an independently scheduled delayed test [16]. A Dutch school trial manuscript could not be retrieved; its reported effect details remain search/index-only and are not endorsed as established findings [17]. Accuracy and latency are proxies that may reflect attention or task format as well as memory [14,16]. Confidence: low-to-moderate for broader adaptive superiority; positive evidence is confined to narrow tasks and source access tiers.

### Tier 3 — Active disputes and outcome boundaries

**D1. Feedback timing has no universal winner.** The feedback review reports task-sensitive results and disagreement about timing [10; R06 evidence table, immediate-vs-delayed row]. In a Saudi undergraduate EFL prose study (n=177), immediate- and delayed-feedback retrieval conditions beat restudy/no-feedback, while immediate feedback exceeded delayed feedback at one week and one month [11]. A different prose-testing source reports a delayed-feedback advantage, but is index-level in this audit [12]. These are not a head-to-head replication; task and timing differ. Confidence: moderate that feedback timing is context-dependent, low for a universal rule.

**D2. Interleaving is not a single treatment.** Reviews report positive math/category-learning findings in some settings and weaker or null results elsewhere [18; R05 evidence table, academic interleaving and middle-school math rows]. A U.S. grade 5–6 tutoring study randomized 269 students (215 analyzed) to blocked, reduced-block, or interleaved fraction practice; after seven days, blocked/reduced-block practice exceeded interleaving on representational knowledge, while operational knowledge did not significantly differ [20; Results, seven-day test]. An abstract-only Indian L2 study reports 44 adolescents in a three-month blocked-vs-interleaved program; its post-test showed no significant blocked/interleaved difference in English vocabulary/grammar retention (p=.17), with no separate post-program delay reported in the abstract [21; ERIC abstract]. The physics course result is a specific counterexample in which interleaving improved delayed novel-problem performance [7]. An IES project page describes a planned systematic replication; it is a protocol/status source, not an outcome result [28]. Ask what is interleaved, after what instruction, and for which test. Confidence: moderate for conditional task effects; low for a universal schedule.

**D3. Generation, self-explanation, and drawing should not be bundled as one mechanism.** The conceptual-understanding review reports positive averages for self-explanation and productive failure, with heterogeneity and moderators [8; R04 evidence table, self-explanation and productive-failure rows]. In the carbon-cycle study, retrieval-only and drawing conditions supported some one-week factual outcomes, while application did not differ [3; Results, one-week delayed test]. Corral et al. tested six experiments with undergraduate psychology students learning to classify true/non-true experiment scenarios (per-experiment recruited samples 269, 300, 310, 246, 196, and 194). Posttests used novel-scenario classification and multiple-choice application/repair questions immediately, after 10 minutes, two days, or one week; classification did not outperform observation, while explanation feedback generally helped [22; Abstract and Experiments 1–6 Results]. Constructive activity labels alone do not establish far transfer. Confidence: moderate for the tested concept/task family; low for generalization to other concepts or populations.

**D4. Transfer findings are more task-specific than retention findings.** Pan & Rickard’s meta-analysis (67 reports, 122 experiments, 10,382 participants) reports an average *d*=.40 (95% CI [.31, .50]) across heterogeneous transfer tests, including changed cues and test formats; that is not equivalent to far transfer across domains [31; R08 Evidence table, retrieval-transfer row]. R08 also summarizes a case-comparison meta-analysis (57 experiments, 336 tests, average *d*=.50); the reported far-transfer estimate is conditional on supplying the principle after comparison, not an overall far-transfer effect [19,32; R08 Evidence table, case-comparison row]. An observational comparison sampled market-working children in Kolkata (N=201) and Delhi (N=400), with Delhi school peers (N=200); task formats/familiarity differed, so it illustrates portability limits rather than a causal instructional effect [23; Results, Figs. 1–2]. The carbon-cycle factual advantage did not extend to application questions [3; Results, one-week delayed test]. Define test changes, prompts, validity, and delay before claiming transfer. Confidence: moderate for near/format transfer in selected tasks, low for broad far transfer.

### Tier 4 — Unknowns and blocked topics

**U1. Mastery learning, forgetting/half-life scheduling, and broad adaptive-difficulty claims cannot be audited from absent review 09.** The adaptive sources here are isolated studies, not an exhaustive comparison of IRT, BKT/DKT, Elo, mastery thresholds, prerequisite graphs, or contextual bandits [14–17]. Predictive accuracy and fitted forgetting curves do not establish a causal delayed-learning benefit. These approaches remain unranked.

**U2. Sleep, circadian timing, stress, exercise, and fatigue are not represented in available reviews 02–08.** Reviews 10–12 are absent, so adequacy of separation between neural findings, biomarkers, and behavioral outcomes cannot be assessed. Biological correlates alone do not establish classroom learning effects.

**U3. Age, culture, language, socioeconomic status, disability, and setting remain under-covered or unquantified.** R02 reports limited non-WEIRD coverage in applied retrieval research; this audit did not re-extract its denominator or classification, and it is a coverage observation, not evidence of effect moderation [1; R02 evidence table, classroom/population row]. A Chinese high-school trial and a small DLD vocabulary study show narrow non-undergraduate settings exist, not that results transport broadly [3,24].

## Crosswalk by evidence status (stable finding IDs)

### Established (at bounded scope)
- **E1:** Retrieval often increases delayed retention in studied tasks; unsuccessful retrieval without correction is not universally useful [1–3].
- **E2:** Spacing benefits many recall settings, while procedural and course-level outcomes can be null or variable [4–6].
- **E3:** Practice scores can move opposite delayed learning, as in the physics course study [7].
- **Cross-cutting (not separately ranked):** Delayed behavioral outcomes must be separated from confidence, engagement, model fit, and physiological proxies; judgments, choices, and measured learning are distinct constructs [12,16,22,26,29].

### Plausible inferences
- **P3:** Use accuracy and response latency as noisy signals for spacing, updating policy against independent delayed outcomes [14–17].
- **P1:** Adapt scaffolding to topic knowledge and fade only when evidence suggests support is redundant [8–9].
- **P2:** Structured generation attempts should be followed by instruction/consolidation; do not infer learning from failure alone [8,13].

### Active disputes
- **D2:** Schedule form, interval, and interleaving effects vary across task, test, prior knowledge, and delay [4,7,18,20–21].
- **D1:** Feedback timing depends on paradigm and content [10–12].
- **D3:** Generation/self-explanation/drawing are distinct actions; activity labels do not establish transfer [3,8,22].
- **D4:** Transfer effects depend on outcome, changed test dimensions, cueing, and delay [19,23,31–32].
- **Cross-cutting:** Mechanisms (retrieval strength, cognitive load, schema formation, consolidation) are not established solely by behavioral differences [2–9].

### Unknowns / unassessed
- Reviews 01, 09–12 and syntheses 13–14 are absent; full-corpus audit is blocked [30].
- Publication bias, overlap among meta-analyses, multi-lab replication, and source-level risk of bias were not reanalyzed.
- Population transportability and long-delay far transfer are not quantified across methods.
- **U1–U3:** Mastery-learning dose/cost, adaptive testing algorithms, biological constraints, contextual bandits, and population-specific effects remain outside or unresolved in this audit.

## Open questions / next research decisions

1. Which simple baselines (fixed spacing, mastery threshold, no adaptivity) should personalized algorithms beat under equal time and exposure?
2. Do accuracy/latency schedules improve independent delayed retention, conceptual explanation, or transfer, rather than only in-app response patterns?
3. Which conditions make retrieval fail, and can success, prior knowledge, task complexity, and feedback be tested factorially?
4. Which interleaving/guidance policies aid discrimination without imposing excess demand on novices?
5. How robust are interventions for adolescents, non-WEIRD learners, disability/neurodiverse groups, multilingual populations, and non-university settings?
6. What do the missing reviews 01/09–12 and syntheses 13–14 conclude? Recover them before claiming full-corpus coverage.
7. Does improved memory generalize to application and far transfer after weeks/months?

## Verification limitations

This remains a partial source audit, not a preregistered systematic review, formal risk-of-bias audit, or independent reanalysis of meta-analyses. Seven project reviews (02–08) were present and read in the parent workspace; the cited local file paths are included in Sources. Some DOI landing pages did not yield extractable content; indexed/abstract records are labeled where used. The publisher DOI page for Corral et al. did not extract cleanly, so its PMC full text is the working source [22]. Inaccessible full texts and index-only sources are identified in Sources. Unresolved quantitative claims are not promoted as established results.
