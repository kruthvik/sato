# Building conceptual understanding: what helps, for whom, and when it fails

**Scope and standard.** This review follows `outputs/research-contract.md`. It treats “understanding” as more than reproducing an example: evidence for conceptual knowledge, explanation, transfer, delayed retention, and procedural performance is distinguished. Instructional formats often combine multiple ingredients, and cognitive-load/schema language is a proposed explanation unless directly measured. Findings from adult laboratory problem-solving do not establish the same effect for children, classrooms, or far transfer.

## Summary

The most consistent pattern is an interaction between instructional support and prior knowledge. Worked examples can help novices learn complex procedures relative to unguided problem solving; fading steps toward independent solving can support the transition. But detailed support may become redundant or harmful for learners with stronger prior knowledge (expertise reversal), and the magnitude depends on task structure and outcome. Self-explanation prompts can improve learning on average, but prompt quality, learner knowledge, and test type matter; the added value of prompts alongside worked examples is not uniform. Analogical/case comparison can help learners notice deep structure, but unsupported comparison may leave the relevant principle unnoticed. Concrete manipulatives often support mathematical learning, especially retention, while transfer effects are smaller; explicit links to drawings/symbols and later practice without the concrete aid matter. Generation/problem solving before instruction can improve conceptual knowledge and transfer when followed by instruction that uses the generated ideas; it is not evidence that unguided discovery is generally superior.

**Adaptive-learning inference:** estimate prior knowledge by topic, not a global ability score. Start with more explicit examples where prerequisite knowledge is low; fade only as learners demonstrate component understanding; offer contrasting cases and explanation prompts where conceptual distinctions matter; allow more independent generation as domain knowledge grows. Measure near and far transfer separately from procedural accuracy. These are design hypotheses to validate in the target domain, not a proven universal sequence.

## What the evidence supports by instructional move

- **Worked examples and example–problem pairs:** most defensible for novices confronting complex, unfamiliar procedures. They reduce immediate search demands; this does not by itself establish conceptual transfer. Pair examples with similar independent problems, then fade.
- **Fading:** a transition strategy, not simply removing help on a fixed timer. Near transfer has more consistent support than far transfer; backward fading and explanation may help some far-transfer tasks but are not universal.
- **Self-explanation:** use focused prompts that elicit relations and justifications. Average meta-analytic benefit coexists with null add-on findings; learner knowledge and prompt design matter.
- **Analogies/contrasting cases:** compare cases to surface relational structure; provide guidance for novices and test application on a new case. Multiple examples without abstraction prompts may not transfer.
- **Concrete-to-abstract:** concrete representations are useful for mathematics, especially retention, but the concrete aid is not the endpoint. Explicitly map to representations and symbols and check performance without the aid.
- **Generation/productive failure:** a carefully designed attempt before instruction may improve conceptual knowledge/transfer; it is followed by instruction and contrast. Do not equate this with unsupported discovery learning.
- **Prior knowledge/expertise:** measure by topic/skill. Guidance can be redundant for knowledgeable learners, but the reversal is moderated, not a universal binary switch.

## Observed findings vs proposed mechanisms

**Observed:** worked examples often outperform unguided problem solving for novices; assistance effects can interact with prior knowledge; case comparisons, self-explanation, concrete representations, and problem-solving-before-instruction show positive average effects in some reviews and domains. Transfer varies and nulls occur.

**Proposed explanations:** limited working memory, schema construction, element interactivity, analogical mapping, retrieval of prior knowledge, and productive failure’s “activation/awareness/consolidation” are explanatory accounts. They are plausible but not proven by outcome differences alone. Cognitive load measures and learner performance are not interchangeable.

## Disagreements and open questions

1. Which part of worked examples causes learning: reduced search, process information, self-explanation, or simply more time on relevant steps? Treatment bundles vary.
2. What is the best criterion and pace for fading, and how should an engine detect when support has become redundant without prematurely withdrawing it?
3. Why do self-explanation and example variability produce nulls in some experiments/meta-analytic subgroups? Prompt quality, prior knowledge, domain, and outcome are candidates but need direct tests.
4. How far do conceptual benefits transfer to unfamiliar contexts and long delays? Many studies use near-transfer or immediate tests.
5. What balance of concrete support and symbolic abstraction works across age, prior knowledge, and math content? Average effects hide variation.
6. Do productive-failure effects generalize to younger learners or domains with low relevant prior knowledge? The evidence does not license universal pre-instruction generation.

## Next research decisions

- Compare worked examples, example–problem pairs, fading, and independent solving with equal time-on-task; stratify by topic-specific prior knowledge and measure delayed near/far transfer.
- Factorially test self-explanation prompts and example variability; score explanation quality and separate conceptual from procedural outcomes.
- Compare concrete→representational→abstract sequencing and explicit mapping against abstract-only teaching, with transfer tests after aids are removed.
- For adaptive engines, treat confidence/response quality and explanation as noisy observations; validate assistance changes in randomized experiments, not just predictive fit.
