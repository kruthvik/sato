# Feedback, errors, and misconception correction in learning

**Scope.** This review follows `outputs/research-contract.md`. Feedback can supply correctness, the correct answer, an explanation, a hint, or a comparison/refutation; timing and frequency are separate design variables. Results on immediate performance are not assumed to imply delayed retention, conceptual revision, or transfer. “Errorful generation” means an attempted answer/problem solution before corrective information; errors alone are not presumed productive.

## Summary

Feedback usually helps when it gives usable information, but “feedback” is not one intervention. Correctness-only signals are weaker on average than answer-plus-explanation in several educational syntheses, though explanation amount should be task-matched rather than assumed to be monotonically better. Timing evidence conflicts across paradigms: immediate feedback is often chosen to avoid carrying an error into subsequent steps (a design rationale), while a delay improved later test performance in some retrieval paradigms. Meta-analyses do not establish one best timing across tasks. Errorful attempts can aid learning when followed by corrective information; high-confidence errors can be corrected particularly well (hypercorrection), but the original error can return if the correction is forgotten. Refutation that names a misconception and explains why it is wrong has better evidence for conceptual revision than bare “incorrect” feedback, but one exposure may not produce durable belief change or changed instructional practice. Optimal feedback frequency is not established across academic learning; much reduced-frequency evidence is motor-specific.

**Adaptive-learning inference:** record response, confidence, prior knowledge, feedback type, and later correction separately. For errors, provide a clear correction and rationale when needed; use a follow-up retrieval/application check and revisit persistent misconceptions after a delay. Use confidence to prioritize monitoring, not as proof that a belief is fixed. Adapt feedback amount to task and learner, and evaluate delayed knowledge revision.

## Synthesis by decision

- **Immediate versus delayed:** Immediate feedback is attractive when errors could propagate or safety/foundational accuracy is at stake. A planned delay can be useful when the learner is retrieving and will then process correction, but existing evidence is mixed across settings. “Delayed” also varies from seconds to days; these should not be collapsed.
- **Answer-only versus explanation:** A correctness signal tells the learner whether to reconsider; answer-only supplies a target; an explanation links the target to a rule or evidence. Explanations are more defensible for conceptual revision and transfer, while concise answer/correction may suffice for simple factual checks. Feedback length is not quality.
- **Confidence-sensitive correction:** High-confidence wrong answers can show large correction after feedback, but if the correction is later forgotten the old answer can return. Low-confidence correct answers may also need consolidation. Confidence is a monitoring feature, not a diagnosis of misconception persistence.
- **Errorful generation/productive failure:** An attempted answer is not the whole intervention. Corrective information, comparison, and later retrieval/application are required to distinguish productive struggle from repeated error.
- **Frequency:** “Every response” vs “summary” findings are largely motor-specific. For classroom or digital conceptual learning, optimal frequency is an open question; test at least one delayed outcome.

## Observed findings vs proposed mechanisms

**Observed:** feedback content and timing vary in effect; retrieval followed by correction can improve later answers; confidence predicts some correction patterns; refutation can improve immediate/average misconception measures; old errors can return; productive-failure designs benefit conceptual/transfer outcomes under specific implementations.

**Proposed explanations:** feedback may direct attention, resolve uncertainty, provide corrective encoding, or trigger surprise; delayed correction may add spacing; productive failure may activate/prioritize prior knowledge and make contrasts salient. These are plausible accounts, not unique mechanisms identified by the outcome comparisons.

## Disagreements and open questions

1. Does immediate feedback prevent reinforcing errors, or does a delay improve retention through spacing/retrieval? The answer likely depends on task, error cost, and whether learners reattempt before correction.
2. How much explanatory detail is optimal for novices versus knowledgeable learners, and when does it overload or distract?
3. Does high-confidence error correction generalize from trivia/factual items to entrenched scientific misconceptions and actual classroom behavior?
4. How often should feedback be delivered in academic tasks? Motor-feedback frequency results do not answer this.
5. Which measures detect misconception change robustly: answer accuracy, explanation, confidence, transfer, or delayed belief/behavior?
6. How should feedback adapt when correctness is right but reasoning is flawed, or when an incorrect response reflects a lucky guess versus a stable misconception?

## Next research decisions

- Factorially vary feedback delay (immediate/brief delay), content (answer only/explanation/refutation), and confidence; test immediate accuracy and delayed correction/transfer separately.
- Compare feedback after every response with summary feedback at equal instructional time in classroom math/science; include delayed no-feedback outcomes and learner-prior-knowledge strata.
- Track misconception recurrence after answer forgetting and test whether spaced retrieval of the refutation reduces return of the original error.
- For adaptive systems, use confidence and response rationale as uncertain signals; validate diagnosis against later explanation and transfer, not a single wrong answer.
