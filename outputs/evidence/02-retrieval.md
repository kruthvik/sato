# Retrieval practice and the testing effect

**Scope.** This review follows `outputs/research-contract.md`. “Testing effect” here means a benefit to later learning from attempting retrieval, compared with an exposure-matched or otherwise specified alternative; it is not simply a higher score on the practice test. Distinguish practice-test accuracy, delayed retention, and transfer. Retrieval format, feedback, success, delay, and comparator matter. Evidence summaries below do not imply that all study contexts or learners benefit equally.

## Bottom line

Across laboratory and classroom research, retrieval practice generally improves delayed retention over restudy/re-exposure, especially when the eventual outcome is recall and practice itself requires retrieval. Restudy can win on an immediate test, so immediate fluency is a poor proxy for durable learning. Classroom meta-analyses find positive average achievement effects, but these aggregate varied interventions and tests; they do not establish uniform transfer to novel tasks. Transfer is positive on average in a meta-analysis but smaller and conditional on response overlap, retrieval success, and elaboration. Free and cued recall often produce stronger delayed recall than recognition practice, but this is not a universal ranking independent of final test format. Corrective feedback is especially important after errors or low-success retrieval. Successive relearning—retrieval to criterion in multiple spaced sessions—has promising course and delayed-retention evidence, though its evidence base is narrower than the general testing effect.

**Adaptive-learning inference:** Model at least item-level delayed success and retrieval format, not just immediate quiz scores. Offer effortful but attainable recall, feedback/correction, and spaced re-retrieval; separately assess application/transfer. Avoid asserting a fixed optimal difficulty or schedule from pooled laboratory findings.

## Synthesis: format, success, feedback, and transfer

**Free recall** offers minimal cues and typically demands more generative retrieval than recognition. **Cued recall** constrains the search while still requiring production. **Recognition** supplies candidate answers and can measure discrimination, but familiarity or cue support may inflate performance relative to unaided recall. These task labels are not a simple difficulty ladder: cue quality, distractors, prior knowledge, and final-test match change the demand. The defensible operational conclusion is to measure the behavior needed later and avoid substituting recognition for recall where unaided production matters.

**Restudy** provides another exposure and can be useful for correcting gaps; it often produces high immediate fluency. **Retrieval** has stronger delayed-retention evidence in many comparisons. A test followed by feedback/restudy can combine retrieval and re-encoding; direct retrieval practice and test-potentiated learning are analytically distinct contributions.

**Retrieval difficulty/success:** the effortful-retrieval account predicts stronger learning when retrieval is challenging, but challenge is not a universal dose-response. If items are too easy, practice may add little; if too hard, learners may fail, guess, or encode errors. The primary evidence favors testing attainable success and feedback rather than maximizing difficulty. “Desirable difficulty” is an explanatory framework, not a directly observed mechanism or universal engine objective.

**Transfer:** retrieval's effect on transfer is smaller and more conditional than the retention result. Similar question formats and overlapping responses are nearer transfer than novel procedures or broad domain application. An adaptive engine should measure factual recall, conceptual explanation, and application separately, and should not infer far transfer from a quiz score.

## Observations vs proposed explanations

- **Observed:** later recall often improves after retrieval relative to restudy; immediate restudy performance can be higher; effects vary with delay, test/practice format, feedback, and success.
- **Proposed mechanisms:** retrieval-induced strengthening/elaboration, contextual cue reinstatement, reduced accessibility of competing answers, attention/strategy changes during later study. These mechanisms are not uniquely established by the behavioral contrast. Meta-analytic effects establish outcome differences, not neural pathways or a single cognitive cause.
- **Competing explanations:** recall-format advantages may reflect greater effort, generation, or better transfer of response processes; format-match/comparator confounds can also account for part of the difference. Test-potentiated-learning accounts emphasize altered later encoding, but do not replace direct retrieval-strengthening explanations.

## Disagreements and open questions

1. Is recall more beneficial because it increases retrieval effort, because it reinstates relational/semantic processing, or because it better matches later tests? The available behavioral comparisons do not cleanly isolate these explanations.
2. How much feedback is necessary after successful versus failed retrieval, and what feedback timing/content best supports conceptual transfer? Moderator results differ between classroom meta-analyses.
3. Which adaptive policy best trades off success probability, effort, time, and retention horizon? Pooled findings do not specify a validated individual-level schedule.
4. How durable are transfer gains on novel real-world tasks, procedures, and diverse language/cultural populations? Coverage remains narrower than factual memory studies.
5. How should retrieval practice be adapted for learners with low prior knowledge, younger ages, or high error costs without encouraging unproductive guessing?

## Next research decisions

- Compare free recall, cued recall, recognition, and restudy with exposure/time matched, then test both matched delayed recall and distinct application outcomes.
- Randomize feedback format/timing and stratify analyses by initial retrieval success and age; include correction retention and error persistence.
- Test successive relearning policies against equal-time alternatives, with prespecified delayed retention horizons and genuinely novel transfer tasks.
- For adaptive engines, log item-level prompt type, response, confidence, latency, feedback, and later performance; test calibration rather than claiming the latent learner state is directly observed.
