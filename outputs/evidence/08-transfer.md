# Transfer and generalization of learning: what moves, how far, and how to test it

**Scope.** This review follows `outputs/research-contract.md`. It separates practiced-item retention from transfer to unpracticed items, changed representations, new problem structures, new contexts/domains, and delayed or real-world use. Near/far labels are reported as used by each source; they are not a universal measurement scale. Transfer-test design and validity are treated as part of the claim, not as an afterthought.

## Summary

Transfer is not one outcome. A learner may remember practiced items, solve new items with the same structure, apply a principle across surface changes, or recognize and use that principle in a different domain or purpose. A transfer claim should specify which dimensions changed—content, context, time, function, modality, social setting, or memory demands—and justify why those changes support the intended inference. Counting changed dimensions alone does not rank difficulty or establish construct validity. Barnett and Ceci’s taxonomy argues that a single far-transfer effect is not meaningful without those details [1].

Evidence rejects both extremes: transfer is neither automatic nor impossible. Meta-analysis of retrieval practice found a moderate average advantage over re-exposure on transfer outcomes (*d*=.40, 95% CI [.31,.50]), but categories ranged from different test formats and related cues to application, problem solving, and untested material initially studied; transfer depended on response congruence, elaborated retrieval, and initial retrieval success [3]. Case comparison also improves performance on later cases on average, but the sizeable far-transfer subgroup effect was conditional on how instruction was sequenced, not a general guarantee from “more examples” [4].

Promising routes include explicit comparison of structurally related examples, naming the shared schema, and varying surface contexts while retaining the target relation. In classic analogy experiments, multiple examples plus comparison supported abstraction and a later semantically distant problem solution [5]; guided comparison of negotiation cases increased use of the represented strategy in new written and face-to-face negotiations [6]. But simply increasing example count or changing surface features is insufficient: effects depend on which features vary, learners’ prior knowledge, and the transfer test [7,8].

The strongest counterweight is context-bound performance. Market-working children in India solved familiar transactions accurately yet struggled with school-like abstract arithmetic, while schoolchildren showed the converse profile on some tasks [9]. These field comparisons are not randomized and differ in format, time, tools, and familiarity; they demonstrate a portability problem, not a clean causal estimate. A medical-physiology classroom experiment likewise found improved trained-concept scores but no advantage of peer instruction over self-explanation on its brief near/far transfer test [10].

**Practical inference:** report at least four outcomes separately: practiced-item retention, unpracticed same-structure items, changed-structure/context transfer, and delayed application. Use multiple independently constructed transfer items, document every training–test difference, and test whether learners can identify the principle without hints. If success depends on familiar wording, a copied template, a prompt, or extra tools, label that assistance rather than overclaiming spontaneous far transfer.

## What “transfer” means—and what tests establish

1. **Practiced-item retention:** the same trained content or item is tested again. This is a learning/retention result, not by itself transfer.
2. **Near transfer:** commonly a new item with substantial overlap in trained representation, procedure, and context. This is an illustrative heuristic, not a reproducible universal threshold; use the source study’s label and report the concrete training–test differences.
3. **Far transfer:** a study-specific label for transfer across more substantial differences, such as domain, setting, purpose, social context, modality, time, or spontaneous recognition. No universal cutoff is used here; describe the actual differences and justify the inference rather than count changed dimensions [1].
4. **Conceptual transfer:** apply a relation, causal principle, or schema to a novel case. **Procedural transfer:** carry a method or sequence to a new item/task. The same intervention can affect these differently.
5. **Preparation for future learning:** prior learning improves learning from new information, even when immediate direct application is not yet possible [13]. This broadens transfer beyond a single post-test, but should be measured separately.
6. **Transfer-appropriate processing:** test performance depends partly on match between the operations practiced and those required at test. That is a valid test-design consideration, but process match is not identical to broad conceptual transfer [11].

A valid transfer claim therefore requires clear evidence that the transfer items were not practiced, a defensible mapping of the invariant principle, explicit description of changed features, and outcome measures that are reliable and appropriate to the population and domain. Include a practiced-item comparator and one or more novel-item sets. Otherwise a gain may reflect item memory, test familiarity, reading load, prompt support, or response-format congruence rather than generalization. This assessment guidance is an inference from the measurement problems documented in the transfer taxonomy, meta-analyses, and classroom studies [1–4,10].

## Synthesis: what seems to promote transfer—and what can block it

### Structural similarity versus surface similarity

In the India arithmetic field studies, familiar goods, routines and presentation accompanied high transaction performance, while performance fell on less familiar or abstract items; because these features were not fully matched, familiarity is a plausible but not isolated explanation [9]. Conversely, Perfetto et al. show that learners did not spontaneously use previously encountered relevant clues without being told of the connection [12]. These task-specific findings support considering cues and context; they do not establish a universal effect of surface resemblance. Analogical mapping and schema approaches predict that explicit comparison helps learners align corresponding roles and abstract a common relation; Gick & Holyoak, Gentner et al., Alfieri et al., Zhang & Zhao, and the Fuchs classroom program support this under specific conditions [4–8]. These findings do **not** imply that all variety helps. Variation that changes several structural rules simultaneously, exceeds prior knowledge, or obscures the invariant can impair learning; novice expertise and instructional support matter.

### Near and far transfer are not a ladder with a single metric

Near/far labels depend on each source’s operationalization; there is no common rule used in this review. A changed test format may count as transfer in Pan & Rickard, while remaining near under a content/domain taxonomy. A same-domain novel application problem can be demanding without changing physical or social setting. Report the actual changes (e.g., new surface context, problem structure, delay, or domain) and justify the inference. Changed-dimension count alone does not establish distance or validity [1,3].

### Conceptual and procedural transfer

Conceptual transfer asks whether learners use an underlying relation or principle; procedural transfer asks whether they can execute a learned method on a new item. Either can succeed without the other. Side-by-side comparison of algebraic solution methods may improve procedural flexibility and performance on new equations without a larger conceptual-knowledge gain than sequential exposure; the present review checked DOI/journal identity in OpenAlex and ERIC, but did not retrieve the primary article, so this claim remains provisional [14]. Conversely, a student may explain a principle but fail to select it spontaneously under time pressure. Test conceptual explanation, method selection, execution accuracy, and unprompted application separately.

### Transfer-appropriate processing and inert knowledge

Test-appropriate processing means that learned operations and test demands interact; it is not evidence that the learner can use the knowledge in an unprompted new domain [11]. Inert knowledge describes a related failure: information may be available in memory but not retrieved when relevant [12]. Transfer tests that include hints or name the relevant principle measure supported application, whereas no-cue tests measure spontaneous noticing plus access plus execution. Both can be educationally important, but they are different outcomes.

### Why context-rich experience can remain local

The India market/school findings caution against assuming that authentic practice automatically transfers to formal notation or, conversely, that abstract school instruction will spontaneously appear in daily practice [9]. Field performance also depends on familiar goods, repeated routines, time pressure, tools, and learned response formats. These data call for matched tasks and mechanism tests, not simple claims that “real-world learning beats school” or that abstract instruction never transfers. Cognitive-training meta-analyses ask a related but distinct question about generalization from training exercises; they should not be treated as direct estimates of transfer from curriculum instruction or example comparison [15].

## Coverage audit

- **Near vs far transfer:** Covered through Barnett–Ceci dimensions, retrieval/case-comparison syntheses, classroom and field examples. “Near” and “far” source-specific definitions are retained.
- **Varied examples / analogical comparison / abstraction:** Covered through meta-analysis, classic lab analogical experiments, negotiation transfer, elementary word problems and classroom schema-broadening.
- **Structural versus surface similarity:** Directly addressed in Zhang & Zhao and through schema-comparison studies; source-specific operationalizations differ.
- **Transfer-appropriate processing:** Covered using Morris et al. as a narrow memory-test illustration, not a general instructional-effect claim.
- **Inert knowledge:** Covered with a primary access-failure example; not a comprehensive review of all inert-knowledge theory or interventions.
- **Conceptual vs procedural transfer:** Discussed through equation-solving comparison literature and conceptual physiology; exact Rittle-Johnson effect sizes were not extracted from primary full text.
- **Transfer-test validity:** Discussed via novelty, item practice, dimensions changed, cueing, delay, response format, reliability and authenticity. Dedicated psychometric reviews and test reliability estimates were not systematically searched.
- **Population/domain:** Evidence spans adult memory/analogy labs, school mathematics, negotiation, medical education, and field arithmetic. No domain-wide effect should be inferred; young children and adults are not interchangeable.
- **Residual limits:** This is a targeted literature review, not a preregistered systematic review. Study quality, overlap among meta-analyses, and publication bias were not independently audited for every synthesis. Some source-specific subgroup figures remain abstract/search-level and are identified as such.

## Open questions and next research decisions

1. Which changes in surface context help learners recognize a schema, and which make it too difficult to retrieve? Test structural variation and surface variation factorially, stratified by prior knowledge.
2. Do explicit comparison prompts improve delayed conceptual transfer, not just immediate strategy choice or practiced-equation performance?
3. How can one separate failure to notice a transfer opportunity from failure to retrieve, failure to execute, and insufficient initial learning?
4. Do transfer effects survive months, new instructors, new settings, and altered social or functional demands?
5. Which transfer tests have adequate reliability and predictive validity for real-world use, and do they introduce reading, cultural, or format demands unrelated to the target skill?
6. When does teaching abstract schemas improve portability, and when does context-rich practice better support performance? Can combined instruction preserve both fluent local skill and cross-context recognition?

**Proposed assessment design (inference):** Randomize learners after a common initial-mastery check to (a) single example, (b) varied examples without comparison, or (c) varied examples with structured comparison and explicit schema naming. Test immediately and after delay using separate, independently authored item sets: practiced items; new same-structure items with surface changes; new structural variants; and a different-domain/functional-context task. Include both cued and spontaneous versions. Have blinded raters score conceptual explanation and procedural execution separately; document response aids/time; report item-level accuracy, uncertainty and test reliability. A separate “preparation for future learning” phase could assess whether the schema helps learners acquire a new but related domain.
