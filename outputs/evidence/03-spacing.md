# Spacing and distributed practice in human learning

**Scope.** This review follows `outputs/research-contract.md`. It distinguishes (i) the interstudy interval (ISI), time between practice events, from (ii) the final retention interval (RI), time between last practice and assessment. “Distributed practice” covers separated learning episodes; “spaced retrieval” adds retrieval attempts. Findings from verbal-learning experiments, classroom interventions, and scheduling-algorithm comparisons are not interchangeable.

## Summary

Spacing versus massing is among the most robust behavioral effects in memory research: across many verbal-recall comparisons, distributed study generally improves delayed retention. The useful interval is not fixed: optimal ISI changes with the intended RI and is non-monotonic (too short or too long can be worse than an intermediate interval). Equal versus expanding schedules have mixed results; advantages depend on total/absolute spacing, first-test timing, and final-test delay, rather than a universal expanding-schedule superiority. Spaced retrieval and relearning are useful combinations, but evidence does not specify a single optimal number or interval for every learner/item. Classroom evidence supports distributed practice on average, but course, age, domain, and schedule details vary. Algorithm evaluations are much thinner than evidence for spacing itself; a scheduler's predictive fit or convenience is not evidence that it improves learning versus another schedule in a controlled trial.

**Adaptive-learning inference:** For memory/recall outcomes, specify the desired retention horizon first; then select candidate review gaps, observe item-specific retrieval outcomes, provide feedback after errors, and evaluate delayed retention separately from understanding, transfer, or procedural fluency. Treat any predicted forgetting curve as uncertain and update it with the learner's observed results rather than a universal timer.

## Lab, classroom, and algorithm evidence are different

**Laboratory:** strongest for isolating spacing conditions, ISI-by-RI interaction, and equal/expanding schedules. Limitations include verbal-memory dominance, controlled study materials, modest transfer distance, and test outcomes often unlike curriculum mastery.

**Classroom:** demonstrates that distributed practice can work in authentic instruction, but designs vary in content, practice opportunities, test delay, and comparator. A pooled classroom estimate should not be interpreted as a universal schedule or extrapolated to every age/domain. Classroom quality and implementation fidelity are essential moderators to inspect in full text.

**Algorithms:** comparative tests of scheduling policies are a smaller literature than spacing research. A model that predicts recall or fits a forgetting curve is not by itself evidence that its schedule improves human learning. Evaluate against simple baselines, equal practice-time budgets, target RI, and delayed/transfer measures; report calibration and subgroup performance.

## Observations vs explanations

- **Observed behavioral pattern:** separated practice often improves later recall relative to massed practice; optimal spacing depends on final test horizon; schedule shape findings vary.
- **Candidate mechanisms:** deficient-processing accounts (massed repetitions receive less attention), encoding-variability/context accounts, retrieval effort/strength accounts, and study-phase retrieval accounts each explain parts of the data. The behavioral results do not uniquely establish any mechanism.
- **Adaptive representation:** item difficulty, exposure history, retrieval success, feedback, and target RI are sensible observable predictors. The inference that they should drive personalized schedules requires validation; a latent “memory strength” should not be treated as directly measured.

## Disagreements and open questions

1. How much apparent equal/expanding advantage is due to total absolute spacing or first retrieval timing rather than schedule shape?
2. What interval optimizes outcomes when learners have different prior knowledge, item difficulty, feedback histories, and retention goals?
3. Do schedules fitted to verbal recall generalize to conceptual understanding, problem-solving, and procedural fluency?
4. Do adaptive algorithms outperform transparent fixed/simple policies under equal time and authentic classroom constraints?
5. How should systems balance long spacing (durability) against retrieval failure, frustration, and the time available before a known assessment?

## Next research decisions

- Factorially randomize target RI, equal/expanding schedule, retrieval success criterion, and feedback; control total practice time and report delayed outcomes.
- Compare simple fixed/Leitner schedules to personalized algorithms in preregistered classroom trials, including out-of-sample calibration and transfer.
- Stratify by age, domain, prior knowledge, and item difficulty; inspect whether an individual's predicted retention curve is calibrated across item types.
