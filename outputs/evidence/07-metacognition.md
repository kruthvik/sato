# Metacognition and study choice: what learners judge, choose, and learn

**Scope.** This review follows `outputs/research-contract.md`. It distinguishes (1) a belief or prediction, (2) calibration/resolution of that belief against performance, (3) a study choice or action, and (4) measured learning—especially delayed retention and comprehension. Judgments of learning (JOLs) are usually item-level predictions of later recall; metacomprehension judgments concern understanding of texts. These are related but not interchangeable. “Study choice” is treated as behavior only when choices or time allocation were observed, not inferred from self-report.

## Summary

Learners’ feelings of familiarity and ease are informative but imperfect. Immediate JOLs can reflect encoding fluency or the visibility of the answer; delayed, cue-only JOLs more strongly predict which paired associates will later be recalled. In a meta-analysis, delayed JOLs substantially improved *relative accuracy* in laboratory memory tasks, but the average memory benefit from making the judgments was small [1]. Delayed judgment is therefore a monitoring aid, not a substitute for a full retrieval attempt [12].

The key gap is between monitoring and control. A learner may identify what feels difficult yet still choose a strategy for reasons besides maximizing delayed learning—diagnosis, speed, enjoyment, or perceived progress. In a four-experiment study, learners often stopped practicing items once they could recall them; they selected further retrieval less often than experimenter-assigned retrieval, and the choices predicted poorer one-week recall [5]. Other flashcard-choice studies show that learners sometimes choose testing even while rating restudy as more effective, partly because they want to diagnose what they know [6]. Thus “belief,” “choice,” and “learning” can diverge.

Rereading and highlighting can increase familiarity and sometimes help memory, but those effects do not license the judgment that material is retrievable or understood [8–10,14]. Meta-analytic evidence supports self-testing and spaced study more consistently than passive rereading [8,14]; learner-generated highlighting improves memory more consistently than comprehension, while instructor-provided highlighting can help both [9]. Note-taking evidence is conditional: the act of note-taking and subsequent review are distinct interventions, and review is important [10,11]. For comprehension, average metacomprehension discrimination is weak, but delayed summary writing and keyword generation can improve it in reviewed studies [4].

**Practical inference for an adaptive learning system:** store predicted confidence separately from correctness, observed choice, latency, and delayed outcome [5–7,17]. Use a closed-book retrieval or explanation attempt—not rereading fluency alone—to estimate accessible knowledge [1,5,16]. When learners choose study actions, record the objective and constraints (diagnose, learn, finish quickly), then compare their chosen policy with later retention [5,6,17,18]. Avoid treating a high JOL or a click on “I know this” as mastery [5,7].

## Concepts and evidence distinctions

- **Calibration (absolute accuracy):** whether a stated probability matches the proportion correct. A learner who says “80% sure” should be correct about 80% of the time across comparable trials [7].
- **Resolution / relative accuracy:** whether higher-confidence items are more likely to be recalled than lower-confidence items, often indexed by a within-person correlation or gamma. Resolution can be good even when the learner is globally overconfident; it is not the same as calibration [1,4].
- **Metacomprehension:** judgment of how well a text has been understood, often assessed against a later comprehension test. An across-text correlation is not a probability calibration measure [4].
- **Study choice/control:** a directly observed decision (e.g., retrieve again, restudy, drop, allocate time, highlight, review notes). A post-hoc report of usual strategy is not an observed choice.
- **Learning outcome:** performance on a specified test at a specified delay. Immediate ease or study-phase success can diverge from later recall, comprehension, and transfer.

## Synthesis: what the evidence does and does not say

### Judgments, calibration, and fluency

JOL accuracy has several meanings. Delayed JOLs improve *relative discrimination* for studied paired associates; this does not mean learners’ confidence probabilities are globally calibrated, nor that they select optimal study actions. In text comprehension, the 2022 meta-analysis found weak baseline metacomprehension discrimination (*r*≈.178), a different construct and evidence base [4]. In both cases, a relationship between confidence and performance is not by itself proof that learners have a correct absolute sense of how much they know [1,4,7].

Fluency is both potentially diagnostic and potentially deceptive. After a cue-only retrieval attempt, response success and latency can carry information about later recall. During rereading, however, visible answers and familiarity may make encoding feel easy without showing that information can be retrieved unaided [2,3,5]. Delaying a judgment can change which cues are available, but a delayed rating may prompt only partial/covert search; Tekin & Roediger’s experiments show it should not be counted as equivalent to overt retrieval practice [1,12,19].

### Study techniques and illusions of competence

Practice testing and distributed practice were rated high utility by Dunlosky et al. because of their generality across materials, learner groups, and criterion tasks; rereading and highlighting were rated low utility in that 2013 review. This is not a claim that rereading/highlighting never help: later syntheses show memory gains in some contexts, and instructor-highlighted texts can support comprehension [9,14]. The crucial distinction is *what* improved: familiarity, fact memory, comprehension, delayed retention, and transfer are different outcomes [8–14].

Note-taking is not simply good or bad. Taking notes can process material and create an external record; reviewing notes can then add opportunities for learning. The combined intervention has support, but the evidence does not license attributing durable learning to transcription alone [10,11]. Likewise, practice testing may serve both monitoring and memory strengthening; learners may choose it for one function while underestimating the other [5,6,16].

### Actual behavior and self-regulated learning

Controlled choices demonstrate that learners do not always maximize delayed learning. Karpicke’s participants often dropped items after a successful first retrieval, while experimenter-assigned repeated retrieval improved one-week recall. Kornell & Son found a different but compatible pattern: in a flashcard task learners’ beliefs favored restudy while their observed choices shifted toward self-testing, which they often described as diagnostic. “Learner chose X” is therefore not evidence that the learner believes X is best or that X produced the best delayed result [5–7,17].

Study regulation also depends on constraints and goals. Choosing an easier or faster action can be rational if the objective is quick completion, feedback about knowledge, or short-term performance [6,17,18]. Online/blended SRL meta-analysis reports a small positive association with achievement, but correlational strategy–grade links do not establish causal study-choice effects. The main open measurement problem is how to observe real study choices over time while still identifying their causal effect on durable learning in authentic courses.

## Coverage audit

- **JOLs and confidence calibration:** Covered via delayed-JOL meta-analysis, cue-utilization research, and calibration/control study [1–3,7]. Calibration and relative resolution are explicitly distinguished. Adult word-pair experiments dominate; school-age calibration evidence is not systematically reviewed.
- **Retrieval and processing fluency:** Covered as a monitoring cue and a potential learning action; cited retrieval/JOL evidence and a major learner-choice experiment [3,5,16,19]. Fluency is not treated as a universal indicator.
- **Illusions of competence:** Covered through overconfidence/premature stopping and the distinction between study fluency and delayed performance. No single “illusion of competence” measure spans all studies.
- **Delayed judgments:** Covered for relative accuracy and for memory reactivity; review includes evidence that delayed JOL is not identical to overt retrieval.
- **Rereading/highlighting:** Covered through a major review and two later meta-analyses; outcomes and moderators separated [8,9,14]. Exact primary trials and publication-bias diagnostics were not exhaustively audited.
- **Note-taking:** Covered using note-taking and note-taking-plus-review meta-analyses [10,11]. Digital vs handwritten methods and domain-specific L2 note-taking were not comprehensively assessed.
- **Self-testing and self-regulated learning:** Covered through direct strategy-choice experiments and one recent online/blended SRL meta-analysis. Most choice results are controlled flashcard/vocabulary tasks, not semester-long naturalistic behavior; SRL–achievement evidence is correlational.
- **Actual study-choice behavior:** Direct choice or allocation evidence is distinguished from self-reported preferences. Learners can show poor stopping policies yet useful item-level test/restudy allocation. Online trace-data SRL literature, developmental trajectories, and classroom choice experiments remain under-covered.
- **Synthesis limitations:** No full systematic search, preregistered screening, study-level risk-of-bias coding, meta-analysis overlap audit, or publication-bias reanalysis was conducted. Accessible summaries/abstracts were used for some reviews; unavailable details are labeled NR/not extracted.

## Open questions and next research decisions

1. In actual school and university courses, do confidence-calibrated learners choose strategies that improve delayed conceptual understanding and transfer, not only paired-associate recall?
2. Can brief scoring rubrics or answer-comparison prompts improve absolute calibration and study choices without increasing time burden or overconfidence?
3. Which naturally logged signals (response accuracy, latency, explanation quality, rereading, highlights, note review) predict delayed learning out of sample—and for whom?
4. Do interventions that teach the testing effect change why learners test themselves (diagnosis vs strengthening), and does that motive predict how they use feedback?
5. How do age, prior knowledge, accessibility needs, subject domain, time pressure, and goal (short-term performance vs durable learning) moderate strategy choice?
6. Does delayed JOL improve retention only when it induces a sufficiently complete covert retrieval attempt, and can that process be measured without conflating monitoring with retrieval practice?

**Proposed experiment (inference):** Randomize learners to usual study choice, delayed cue-only JOL, or explicit closed-book retrieval plus criterion feedback. Track item-level confidence, correctness, response latency, choice and time on rereading/highlighting/notes/testing. Test immediate, one-week and one-month comprehension/transfer. Pre-register separate monitoring outcomes (calibration and resolution), action outcomes (choice/time), and learning outcomes; stratify by age/prior knowledge and report attrition and uncertainty.
